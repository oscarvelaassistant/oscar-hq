## 1. Executive summary

**The answer.** Pocket Factory is a real gap with paying demand on one side and a working brain on the other, and nobody has yet joined them in a box. The gap is narrower than the Mars story and more concrete: a cell where an AI agent runs fabrication machines and two arms move the part between them, from a text request to an accepted part, with no skilled operator inside, at fab-lab prices. The first paying buyer is a navy or an expeditionary unit, because they already spend on it. In its present form (a solo, non-technical founder, no hardware, a simulated demo) it is not fundable at pre-seed by the funds that say what they need; it is fundable by angels and a pre-seed only once two things exist: a technical cofounder and a filmed first physical run, or a signed letter of intent from a buyer. The simulated demo is the right next step anyway, because it is free, it proves the design is coherent, and it is what recruits the cofounder.

**Key findings.**

1. The demand is paid for today. The US Navy's FY2026 budget carries 196.585M USD for afloat and deployable manufacturing (https://www.highergov.com/budget/navy-advanced-manufacturing-5483f47). APFIT awarded 30M USD in May 2026 for five containerised print cells (https://www.tectonicdefense.com/exclusive-firestorm-snags-30m-apfit-contract/). At RIMPAC 2026 a deployable hybrid cell made 12 brackets for 2,492 USD against a 9,624 USD shipyard quote (https://www.tctmagazine.com/snowbird-technology-reports-cost-savings-on-4-parts-3d-printed-during-rimpac-2026-exercise/).
2. Every deployable factory sold today ships with people inside: two engineers in the Army container (https://singularityhub.com/2013/02/28/3d-printing-on-the-frontlines-army-deploying-2-8m-mobile-fabrication-labs), a 15-day course for SPEE3D operators (https://www.spee3d.com/metal-parts-on-the-frontlines-spee3ds-impact-on-ukrainian-military-operations/), QinetiQ staff for a year at HMNB Clyde (https://www.gov.uk/government/news/additive-manufacturing-transforming-submarine-maintenance-and-support). The operator is the cost and the bottleneck.
3. The operator is becoming software. Frontier models drive real arms zero-shot (Apple AMP1, 85.6 percent on RoboLab-120 against 49.2 for the best trained policy, https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09892) and their robot-control ability doubled every 53.9 days from July to October 2026 (https://lesswrong.com/posts/ndS9Xn8Qx5PppHJ3Q/the-ability-of-frontier-models-to-control-robots-is-rapidly). They are slow, about 30 minutes per task against 20 seconds for a human (https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/aa-robotics).
4. The closest competitor, MicroFactory, is three people, 1.5M USD raised, sells an assembly cell from 11,950 USD, runs customer production on frontier-model-written code, and states the same endgame (`microfactory-catalogue.md`, `ihorbeaver-sweep.md`). It does not fabricate and does not sell into sites without resupply. It could add a printer module quickly.
5. No investor statement found says a simulation-only hardware demo is enough at pre-seed. HAX lists "concept stage without a working prototype" and "a sole founder working with contracted engineers" as rejection reasons (https://hax.co/faq/). Every disclosed pre-seed above 1M USD in the comparables table had a physical prototype, paid pre-orders, a pilot, or founders from Tesla, Boston Dynamics, DeepMind or Imperial. Carta: solo founders were 35 percent of 2024 startups and 17 percent of those that closed a VC round (https://www.fundssociety.com/en/news/alternatives/las-startups-dirigidas-por-un-fundador-individual-se-han-mas-que-duplicado-pero-tienen-menos-exito-a-la-hora-de-conseguir-capital-riesgo/).

**Recommendation (mine).** Wedge: repair parts at the point of need for vessels and forward sites, with a navy or expeditionary unit as the first buyer and a European coast-guard or naval route through what Oscar knows, once the ARESA contract question is answered. Demo: "bar stock in, accepted part out", in MuJoCo from CAD, on a real navy part list (the USS Essex and RIMPAC parts), with the agent's telemetry next to the render. Deck: lead with the three facts above, show the demo, and answer "when does it run on real metal" with a dated plan and a named technical cofounder. Raise: angels and a small pre-seed, not VC, until the first physical run exists. The 30 days below cost nothing.

## 2. The question and the hypothesis tree

**Question.** Is Pocket Factory fundable at pre-seed within the path Oscar chose (no spend, simulated demo, deck, investor), and what has to be true for that?

Each leaf below is tested against evidence in this report, with the outside judge that would settle it beyond this document.

- **H1. A buyer pays today for parts made at the point of need.** Supported. Navy budget line, APFIT, Royal Navy, UK MoD strategy (section 3). Judge: one named supply officer or innovation office confirms a part list and a price they paid. Not yet done.
  - H1a. The buyer's pain is lead time and downtime, not part cost. Supported by the Navy: parts that "could not be purchased" (https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/uss-essex-prints-its-own-parts-at-sea-rimpac-2026-253121/) and by Firestorm: repair parts before new products (https://x.com/Firestorm_Labs/status/2090046283088380365). Judge: a repair log with lead times from a real vessel or yard.
- **H2. What exists today leaves a gap.** Supported. Robot cells assemble, fabrication machines have no hands, deployable factories need people inside (`market-map.md`, section 4). Judge: a competitor shipping an operator-free fabrication cell would falsify it; none found on 13 Nov 2026.
- **H3. The operator can be software within the time an investor cares about (2 to 3 years).** Partly supported. Zero-shot control works and improves fast; it is slow and fails on contact-rich tasks (section 5). Judge: a filmed run on a real part with a pass rate and a time per part, scored by someone other than Oscar.
- **H4. A cell can be built in the fab-lab price class, not the Army-container class.** Open. Components are priced (arms 2,400 to 5,473 EUR, printers 509 to 3,499 USD, desktop CNC 1,399 to 5,799 USD, `market-map.md`), the integration is not. Judge: a bill of materials with quotes from the hardware-engineer role.
- **H5. Oscar, as he is today, can raise on this.** Not supported for VC; open for angels. Section 7. Judge: ten investor conversations with written reasons.
  - H5a. A simulated demo substitutes for a prototype. Not supported by any investor statement found. Judge: the same ten conversations.
  - H5b. A technical cofounder or a signed letter of intent changes the answer. Supported by the comparables pattern (section 7) and by HAX's stated rules. Judge: the cofounder exists, or the letter does.
- **H6. The ARESA contract allows this.** Unknown. Nothing in the repo records the clauses. Judge: the contract text, read by Oscar, and if needed a lawyer. Everything else waits on this.

What must be true for a yes at pre-seed, in one line: H1 confirmed by a named buyer, H2 still open, H3 shown once on real metal, H5b in place, H6 answered.

## 4. Competition landscape

### 4a. MicroFactory, first and in depth

Facts from `microfactory-catalogue.md` and `ihorbeaver-sweep.md`, URLs there. Founded 2024 in San Francisco by Igor Kulakov and Viktor Petrenko, three people, Founders Inc portfolio. Pre-seed 1.5M USD at a 30M USD post-money cap, September 2025, on the back of a launch video with 723,000 views and 100+ paid reservations at about 5,000 USD. Product today: a boxed cell with one or two Fairino FR3 industrial arms (3 kg payload, 0.02 mm repeatability), hot-swap grippers, stereo cameras, a light panel, from 11,950 USD single to 31,900 USD dev kit; customers pay Anthropic or OpenAI tokens for setup and get a five-year local-model licence for running. The "AI integrator" (Astra and Opus) designs tooling, writes vision code and guides the operator. Production evidence: Unruggable paid 20,000 USD and runs 2.5-hour autonomous shifts at one gadget per 7 minutes. Stated endgame: a self-replicating factory, US reindustrialisation, other planets as a side product.

What they changed in 18 months: price from about 5,000 USD to 21,900 USD for two arms; arms from custom harmonic-drive builds to bought-in industrial arms, because 3D-printed parts "still need CNC work after" and the model "needs harmonic motors" to work. What they do not do: cut, print, mill or weld as a first step; sell to sites without resupply; Europe. What they say about Oscar's path: Isaac Sim "doesn't work well for simulating precise tasks like electronics assembly" (Petrenko), and the model needs stiff arms (Kulakov). Where Oscar should expect them next: a printer module is one weekend of work for them; a container is "a good product" in Kulakov's words. The bench is theirs. The fabrication loop, the no-resupply brief, and Europe are open.

## 5. Technology readiness: what frontier models plus simulation can and cannot do today

Facts, from `sim-demos-scan.md` and tonight's search:

- Design is solved enough to demo. Fable 5.2 designed three working devices (spec, 4-layer PCB, enclosure, firmware) that Diode built with no circuit fixes (https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-electronics-design); a 689-part humanoid was designed overnight in build123d and balanced in MuJoCo (https://x.com/earthtojake/status/2120247469900800316); text-to-cad, 18,000+ GitHub stars, now exports to MuJoCo with mass and inertia from the CAD (https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad).
- Control is real and slow. AMP1: 85.6 percent on RoboLab-120 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09892). AA-Robotics on a real arm: GPT-6 Astra 69.4, Claude Fable 5.1 46.3, about 30 minutes per task (https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/aa-robotics). Anthropic's own evaluation: direct control of a Franka in LIBERO 0 to 5.5 percent, all models failed a real hallway loop (https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-plays-robotics). Gemini 4 Argon self-improved an SO-101 pick-and-place from 58.9 s to 17.4 s (https://developers.googleblog.com/en/gemini-4-argon-for-robotics/). A real-arm study halted trials after unsafe actions damaged equipment (https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24170).
- Sim-to-real breaks at contact. A digital twin tracked free-space motion within 0.2 to 0.4 degrees but success on replayed contact tasks fell to about half (https://x.com/ja_rothschild/status/2120841577933197413). "The sim assumed zero tolerance, and you're back to calipers" (https://x.com/pggalaviz/status/2120289078310584589). MuJoCo and PhysX do not model cutting forces, chip formation, tool wear, thermal growth or solder behaviour; industrial CNC twins use the controller vendor's real machine data (`sim-demos-scan.md`, 3.3).
- The production pattern that works is constrained: fixed frame, fixed cameras, fixtures, the frontier model for setup and code, small local models or code at runtime (MicroFactory; Kulakov's System 1 and System 2 post, https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/2110866012333838675).

Reading (mine): a simulated demo can prove that the design is coherent, the agent pipeline runs end to end, and the cell's geometry works. It cannot prove reliability, accuracy, speed or cost, and for a factory product those four are the business. The deck should say exactly this, before an investor does.

## 6. Inspiration: what people build with Fable and in simulation, and what to borrow

From `sim-demos-scan.md`, 34 examples read, all with URLs there. The stack that produced the most-viewed demos: a prompt at max effort, CAD as build123d code exported to STEP, physics in MuJoCo with masses from the CAD, film cut in Blender from real sim runs, plus a single-file three.js twin an investor can click. Builder rules worth adopting: export 3MF so print settings travel with the part; put tolerances in the model because the sim will not; let the model write the controller as code and keep motion in code; design the cell to a bare interface (cameras plus an HTTP API, the AA-Robotics shape) so any model can drive it and every run is logged.

What to borrow for the demo (opinion, mine, agreeing with Innovator's top pick in `sim-demos-scan.md`): "bar stock in, accepted part out". Two arms load stock into a desktop mill or printer, swap tools, deburr, measure with a simulated probe, and fit the part to a fixture, all in MuJoCo from the CAD, with the agent's telemetry on screen next to the render. Make the parts real: the oxygen-tank brackets and thermostat covers that US ships printed at RIMPAC 2026 (https://www.tctmagazine.com/snowbird-technology-reports-cost-savings-on-4-parts-3d-printed-during-rimpac-2026-exercise/; https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/uss-essex-prints-its-own-parts-at-sea-rimpac-2026-253121/), and the Coast Guard strainer the Marines printed for 12 USD (https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/marine-corps-x-fab-saves-coast-guard-patrol-boats-253447/). A demo on parts a navy actually needed is a different slide from a demo on a fidget spinner. Second scene: the cell makes its own gripper finger and swaps it in. Closing film only: the container unboxed at a remote site, because that is where a render looks most like a render.

Do not design the arms. Use MuJoCo Menagerie models (https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_menagerie) and published arm files; the design work is the cell, the fixtures, the tool dock and the recipe library, which is also where the company's value sits.

## 7. Investor lens

### 7a. How investors treat sim-only hardware proofs of concept

No named fund found says a simulation-only demo is enough at pre-seed for a hardware product; every one that addresses it asks for a built thing or a buyer. HAX/SOSV: "concept stage" without a working prototype and "a sole founder working with contracted engineers who have no equity stake" are listed rejection reasons; the deal is 250,000 USD with a prototype expected (https://hax.co/faq/). firstminute capital: "Simulations are helpful but not a panacea ... Build with a customer as soon as possible even if that means using commodity tech" (https://www.firstminute.capital/news-announcements/our-robotics-thesis). Cybernetix Ventures: "A working demo is not enough ... Real validation is a pilot customer who places a second purchase order", and they prioritise "founders who've spent years inside the industry they're now trying to automate" (https://www.robotics247.com/article/the-venture-capital-and-investment-view-from-inside-robotics-defining-decade). Salesforce Ventures' checklist asks whether the demo is "staged, sped up, teleoperated, or overly polished" and "What's the real-world vs. simulated data split?" (https://salesforceventures.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/25_EA_SFV_Checklist_Investing_Final.pdf). Sky9: "A single authentic field deployment story is more persuasive than a strong simulation result" (https://www.sky9capital.com/blog/investors-ai-robotics-embodied-ai/). Bolt's standard: signed letters of intent and "a solid functional prototype that users can take out of a box" (https://blog.bolt.io/?p=162). A 2026 robotics fundraising guide, its own claim: the "smart team and a sim" round "is now called pre-seed and it caps closer to 1.5M USD" (https://hub.causo.ai/guides/raising-seed-robotics-startup-2026). YC accepts ideas (40 percent of each batch) and solo founders, says most businesses need a technical cofounder, invests 500,000 USD, and its Winter 2027 request wants robots and their supply chain built in the US (https://ycombinator.com/faq; https://www.ycombinator.com/rfs?year=2025).

Funded without hardware, for contrast: Machenta (40M USD seed, sim-trained research, founder runs Imperial's robot-learning lab), Genesis AI (105M USD seed, ex-Mistral and CMU), The Bot Company (150M USD, Cruise founder), Antioch (4.24M USD pre-seed, product is a simulation platform). None was a non-technical solo founder, and none sold a hardware product.

## 8. Recommendation for this stage (mine throughout)

**The wedge.** Repair parts at the point of need for vessels and forward sites. Not "a factory for Mars", not "a Shenzhen in a pocket", not soldering. It is the only segment where a complete deployable cell has been bought with public money in the last two years, the pain is lead time rather than part cost, and Oscar's insider knowledge (a yard that builds patrol vessels for navies and police) points at it. The sentence for the deck: "A ship at sea is the nearest thing on Earth to a place with no supply chain. We build the cell that makes the part the ship is waiting for, with nobody inside the box."

**The demo spec.** In MuJoCo from build123d CAD via text-to-cad, free. Scene 1: a text request for a real navy part (the RIMPAC oxygen-tank bracket) arrives; the agent designs it, checks it, plans the toolpath; two published arm models load stock into a desktop mill module, swap tools, deburr, probe, and fit the bracket to a fixture; telemetry (joint error, cycle time, pass or fail) shown live beside the render. Scene 2: the cell makes and fits its own gripper finger. Scene 3, closing film only: the container at a remote site. Deliverable: a two-minute film cut from real sim runs plus a browser twin the investor can click. Judge: the twin runs the same sequence on three parts the author did not design, scored pass or fail by someone other than Oscar and Claude.

**What the deck must prove, slide by slide.** The three lead facts (demand paid, people inside every box, the operator became software). The gap map (section 4b). MicroFactory named and respected, with the three things it does not do. The demo, with its limits stated by us first. The dated path to real metal: the Stage 1 bench from `plan.md`, with its quotes (arm pair 226 EUR, PiPER 2,400 EUR, printer 509 EUR), as "what the first money buys". The team slide with a named technical cofounder, or the honest line "cofounder search in progress, here is the profile". The ask: angels and a pre-seed in the 250,000 to 1.5M USD range that the comparables show for this stage, not a VC seed.

**Who to pitch first.** Angels and operators in robotics and defence, YC (accepts ideas and solo founders), HAX only once a prototype exists, the ESA BIC when the 2027 call opens if the space link is written in. Spanish deeptech funds after the first physical run. Every approach is Oscar's own and is a gate 1 action for the company.

**Risks, plainly.**
- The ARESA contract. IP, non-compete and outside-work clauses are unknown to this repo. The first customers overlap with the employer's customers. Read before anything else; nothing in this report should reach anyone before that answer. If the yard owns what he builds at home, the wedge changes to fab labs and small fabricators and the ladder stays.
- Solo, non-technical founder. The data in 7d and HAX's rule say this is the single largest discount on the round. The simulated demo is the recruiting tool for the cofounder, and should be built as one.
- Capital intensity. Each rung in `plan.md` costs about an order of magnitude more than the last. A rung started before the previous one has paid is how this dies. The Formic model (fee per month, 97 percent renewal) is the way a hardware cell earns like software.
- MicroFactory. Fifteen months ahead on the cell, paying customers, the same endgame, a weekend from a printer module. Compete only on what he does not do; keep buying his cell as an option for the operator.
- Sim-to-real. Contact halves success; the sim does not know cutting forces. Say it first.
- Defence as first buyer. Export control, procurement rules, and a sales cycle a solo founder on a day job cannot run. The regulation-procurement role maps it before any approach.
- Machinery regulation. A cell on a floor is a machine under EU Regulation 2023/1230, applying from 20 January 2027 (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1230/oj).

**Kill criteria.** Ten investor conversations and no second meeting with the demo and a cofounder in hand; or no navy, coast guard or expeditionary contact names a part they waited for; or the contract forbids it and cannot be renegotiated.
