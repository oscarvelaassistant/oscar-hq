## 2. What I think (my opinion)

**Oscar's instinct about MicroFactory is right, and it points at the real product.** Two arms that assemble parts you bought elsewhere are a lab appliance. A factory takes raw material in and puts an accepted part out. Cofounder's scan found the same thing: nobody closes "raw material to finished part" on one bench, and the one field signal on demand says the first thing people want is spare and repair parts at the point of need (Firestorm Labs, https://x.com/Firestorm_Labs/status/2090046283088380365). The US Navy says the same from the other side: USS Essex now runs printers that do additive and subtractive work at sea, making thermostat covers and oxygen-storage components that "could not be purchased," and its supply officer calls the point "the entire manufacturing process to occur on the ship" (https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/uss-essex-prints-its-own-parts-at-sea-rimpac-2026-253121/). USS Bataan has carried a metal printer since 2022 to make repair parts for a ship commissioned in 1997 (https://maritime-executive.com/article/amphib-uss-bataan-can-now-print-spare-parts-in-stainless-steel). The demand for a factory with no supply chain behind it is real, and it is paid for today.

**One system that solders and also makes big things does not mean a giant robot.** It means the arms are the operator and the factory is the set of machines they tend. A fitter in Oscar's yard tends a saw, a press brake and a welder; nobody expects the fitter's hands to cut steel. A standard fab lab holds a laser cutter, a 3D printer, a precision mill that makes circuit boards and moulds, a large router "for building furniture and housing," and an electronics bench, for 25,000 to 65,000 USD of equipment plus 15,000 to 40,000 USD of consumables, or about 120,000 USD for the whole standard inventory (https://fabfoundation.org/getting-started/). Every one of those machines is a candidate module. The fab lab network, 1,750 labs in more than 100 countries by that page, proved two things in twenty years: the machines travel easily, and the skilled operator does not. That operator is what became software in the last eighteen months (openpi, https://github.com/Physical-Intelligence/openpi; Gemini Robotics 1.5, https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03342; Apple's AMP1 harness driving arms from a frontier model, https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09892). The Pocket Factory is the fab lab with the operator built in. Soldering is the operator's first fine-motor skill; a router bed is its first big one.

**Bigger than the box, three honest ways.** (a) Modules with a larger work envelope than the arms, and arms on a rail so they reach the module's bed. (b) Assembly: parts the cell made, joined into structures larger than the cell, the way Reframe Systems' workcell makes building panels (landscape.md). (c) Self-extension: the factory builds its next, bigger machine from stock, the RepRap principle (https://reprap.org/wiki/RepRap), which is also what Almond does when it uses Axol to make Axol parts (landscape.md). Route (c) is the only one that reaches Mars, and it is also the one that makes a village factory grow instead of wear out.

**Mars and the remote village share one design constraint: no resupply.** That constraint, not the location, is what to design for. The US Army's Expeditionary Lab put 3D printers, milling machines, laser, plasma and water cutters and welding gear in a 20-foot container with a generator and satellite link, run by two engineers, at about 2.8 million USD per container (https://singularityhub.com/2013/02/28/3d-printing-on-the-frontlines-army-deploying-2-8m-mobile-fabrication-labs). Firestorm's xCell is the 2026 version, run at sea aboard USS Essex (https://www.dvidshub.net/news/464611/its-basically-expeditionary-3d-printer). The ambition in one line: the same container, no engineers inside, at a small fraction of that price, able to repair itself. That is a company. "Two arms in a box" is a product line inside it.

**Three corrections from the 10:09 version, kept and shortened.** (1) The framing is "remote village," and that is right. Suame Magazine in Kumasi supports over 200,000 livelihoods doing metalwork and spare parts by hand (https://openair.africa/skills-development-knowledge-and-innovation-at-suame-magazine-kumasi/); what remote places lack is precision, drawings, repeatability and feedstock, not the will to build. Design for feedstock and power, and keep the Rhodes line out of every room. (2) The general robot brain is the capital trap (Skild AI at 14 billion, https://pulse2.com/skild-ai-1-4-billion-funding/; Physical Intelligence at 5.6 billion, https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-20/robotics-startup-physical-intelligence-valued-at-5-6-billion-in-new-funding). Those companies release weights and need tasks, data and paying customers. The Pocket Factory is the recipe library plus the modules plus the acceptance tests; the brain is rented. (3) Oscar's insider knowledge is the shipyard. Aresa builds aluminium and composite vessels up to 60 m, patrol boats for navies and police (https://www.naval-technology.com/contractors/patrol/grup-aresa-internacional/). New in this revision: a ship at sea is the nearest thing on Earth to a habitat with no resupply, and Aresa builds the ships. A fabrication cell that lives aboard a patrol vessel or at a forward base is a customer for the container stage that exists today. The yard bench is the first rung, and the yard's customers are a later one. My opinion, and every approach to any of them is gate 1 and needs the employer question settled first.

Against his own record (closed ventures: software too easy to copy, boring work, missing insider knowledge): hardware, not boring, insider knowledge present through the yard. The bigger end state does not change the ninety-day test; it changes what the test is for.

**The closest competitor is further along than "the floor" suggested (added 11:10).** The sweep changes the picture of MicroFactory from a limited soldering box to a company on the same ladder with a fifteen-month head start: toy simulation in July 2024, a sold DevKit in November 2025, a named model, an AI integrator built on Opus and Astra, and customer deployments where frontier-LLM-written code runs a gadget line for hours (`ihorbeaver-sweep.md`, section 1). His stated endgame is a self-replicating factory and other planets, and he has said a container factory "sounds like a good product." What he does not do, by his own posts and site: he assembles parts the customer supplies, with final assembly of a new cell from off-the-shelf components as his self-replication v1 (https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/2110866870215782551); nothing on the account cuts stock, mills, prints or welds as the first step; his market is dense hubs, the Bay Area as the US Shenzhen (https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/2024693339455705550), not sites with no resupply. So three things are ours to own, and only one of them is a bench: (1) raw stock to accepted part on one bench, the fabrication modules and the acceptance tests; (2) the no-resupply constraint as the design brief (ship, forward base, village) with the demand log that comes from it; (3) Europe: a CE path under Regulation 2023/1230, naval and coast-guard customers reachable through the yard, and the Fab network as the village channel. The honest caveat: he can add a printer module to his cell in a weekend, his own thread already lists "3D printing" among the specialised cells. A printer on a bench is not a moat. The field-hardened container, the recipe library tied to real repair logs, and the customers are. One more option the sweep opens, my opinion: he may be a supplier as much as a competitor. If Stage 3 needs an industrial-grade operator, buying his single-arm cell (11,950 USD, https://microfactory.com/) or a bare Fairino FR3 (quoted below) and building the fabrication modules and recipes around it is cheaper than rebuilding what he has already debugged. That is a Stage 3 quote, parked until then.

**The arm question, answered plainly (added 11:10).** Kulakov's position is that the model only works on stiff industrial internals: "We need harmonic motors for our model to work" (https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/2073186007718256669), VLAs generalise from little data "if you run them on good hardware" (https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/2013466973007425936), and his own first 3D-printed arm failed USB insertion from shake, not from datasheet precision (https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/1864429517064032295). The SO-101 is a 3D-printed arm on six STS3215 bus servos with 1/345 gearing, 229.88 USD or 226.30 EUR for a leader and follower pair, and its documentation states no payload or repeatability figure at all (https://huggingface.co/docs/lerobot/so101, https://github.com/TheRobotStudio/SO-ARM100). That is the whole point: nobody has measured what this plan would need from it, so Stage 1a measures it in week one. What the SO-101 can prove: that Oscar can run LeRobot, teleoperate, collect demonstrations and fine-tune (the SmolVLA numbers were produced on this arm's predecessor); that the bare interface, the agent loop and the recipe library work; that a printer-tending and deburring cycle runs; a yard task within the arm's tolerance. What it cannot credibly prove at a pass rate: through-hole soldering at 90 percent of joints, press fits, anything with sub-millimetre tolerance. Those pass lines move to Stage 1b, one stiff arm, bought when 1a's logs show the arm is the limit and not the agent. Quoted candidates, 13 Nov 2026: AgileX PiPER, 1.5 kg payload, ±0.1 mm repeatability, 626 mm reach, 2,400 EUR incl. VAT at Génération Robotics (https://www.generationrobots.com/en/404258-6-axis-robotic-arm-piper.html) or 3,556.95 EUR incl. VAT at MYBOTSHOP (https://www.mybotshop.de/AgileX-Piper_6), gripper not included; UFACTORY Lite 6, 600 g payload, ±0.5 mm, 440 mm reach, from 2,999 USD (https://www.ufactory.cc/lite-6-collaborative-robot); Fairino FR3, the arm inside MicroFactory's cell, 3 kg payload, ±0.02 mm, 622 mm reach, 5,473 EUR incl. VAT (https://www.welectron.com/Fairino-FR3-Robot-Arm). My pick for 1b is the PiPER at the Génération Robotics price: an order of magnitude stiffer than a hobby arm at an order of magnitude less than the industrial class. Kulakov's own path was the same ladder in miniature: a printed arm first, a redraw when real tasks exposed the shake, industrial internals by the time customers paid.

**"I can catch up in no time": true for half of it (added 11:10).** For: Kulakov came from game design and indie software, and reached paying robot deployments in about fifteen months, with a frontier model designing tooling, writing vision code and guiding the operator (https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/2104646736652447854); Apple's AMP1 drives arms from a frontier model by writing Python; LeRobot ships the whole imitation-learning stack open; openpi and SmolVLA are free. The software half of robotics in 2026 is agent engineering, which Oscar does daily. Against: the same founder says hardware quality is a precondition for the AI, not something it compensates for; his redraw took weeks of printing and testing; a frontier model shortens design and code, and does nothing for shipping times, print hours, fifty demonstrations collected by hand, a dial indicator reading, or a CE file. So the schedule below puts every LLM-accelerated piece before the hardware arrives (harness, simulated runs, the CAD-from-photo pipeline tested on the Stage 0 repair log, fixture and tool-dock designs ready to print) and sets a hard target that assumes the catch-up is real: the first filmed raw-stock-to-fitted-part run within fourteen days of the hardware arriving. If that target is missed, the miss and its reason are the finding.

## 3. Ambition framing (be-ambitious, Felix Rieseberg's three types)

- **Goal beyond the goal:** a factory that runs where it is dropped, and can make its own next part.
- **Types used:** Altitude (this serves an outcome: a fundable company and a working container, not a document), Depth (robotics founders who get funded show measured runs, not slides; the rawest material is the yard's own repair log), Range (expeditionary fabrication, fab labs, naval supply, in-situ resource use and an artisan each know something the robotics field does not). Speed skipped, as the skill says.
- **Altitude ladder:**
  1. One bench that turns raw stock into an accepted part and also solders, on the same arms and the same agent. This alone passes MicroFactory's gap.
  2. One cell that makes something bigger than itself, tends its own modules unattended, makes one of its own spare parts, and sells repair parts per hour of output.
  3. One container that runs thirty days off-grid with a local operator who was not trained by us, makes the parts list a site asked for, and repairs itself once. After that, the factory that builds its next machine.
- **Depth (Felix's four questions):** What a top founder would do that was not asked: skip the deck, film the bench making and fitting fifty repair parts, and publish the pass rate. Rawest material: the yard's log of parts it waited for last quarter (part, lead time, cost, downtime), fitters' bench tasks timed by hand, and a ship's or a village's list of things that break. Methods and tools: LeRobot teleoperation and imitation learning for fine tasks, a frontier-model harness in the AMP1 style for setup and planning, CAD generation from photo and dimensions, a fixed fifty-trial protocol on unseen parts behind an AA-Robotics style bare interface (cameras plus an HTTP API, https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/aa-robotics). Hard targets below, treated as reasonable. Outside judge: a fitter accepts or rejects each part against a drawing; a second person counts on video. Added 11:10, two Depth moves from the sweep: measure the SO-101's repeatability and settling time with a dial indicator and the cameras before any task, and publish the number next to every pass rate; and run the same yard task two ways, a SmolVLA fine-tune and frontier-model-written code with a small aligned dataset (Kulakov's method, https://x.com/ihorbeaver/status/2110866012333838675), so the belief "VLA versus code" is tested on our bench rather than argued.
- **Range (the verbs):** "turning what is on site into an accepted part, with no skilled person and no supply chain, anywhere." The crew and what each does: an expeditionary-fab engineer (the Army lab and Firestorm: write the consumables list and the no-engineer failure modes before building anything); a ship's supply officer (price the cell against the lead time of the part it replaces, in days of downtime, not in robot hours); a fab-lab veteran (measure skilled minutes saved, not machines shipped; pick modules from the standard inventory because their recipes already exist); a Suame artisan (what they would pay for is one drawing turned into ten identical parts); an ISRU engineer (ESA and DLR sintered brick-sized blocks from lunar soil simulant in a solar furnace, roughly as strong as gypsum, five hours per brick, https://www.space.com/36943-make-lunar-soil-bricks-solar-heat.html; the lesson is that feedstock is the long pole on Mars, so the factory's design separates "feedstock in" from "part out" from day one); a short seller as red team (what kills this: field reliability, capital per scale step, certification, and a founder with one hour a day); added 11:10, a robot-cell founder who already ships (Kulakov's practice list in `ihorbeaver-sweep.md`, section 4: prove the task by teleoperation first, make one awkward insertion the reference task, get rid of shake before scaling, constrain the environment, build a jig for the awkward step, buy the 300 USD screw feeder, swap the whole tool-fixed gripper; each of these is a line in the Stage 1 protocol, not a quotation).

## 5. The roadmap

Durations are my estimates. Each stage has a test, a pass line, a cost, the gate it crosses and a kill criterion.

### Stage 0: Thesis, wedge and the repair log (now to 2026-11-30)
- Do: a one-page `thesis.md` in this folder: first customer, first task, why now, what the box is at each rung. Two lists from the yard floor, phone notes, no names: ten bench tasks done by hand (skill, minutes), and ten parts the yard waited for last quarter (part, lead time, cost, what stood still). Read the employment contract for IP, non-compete and side-activity clauses before anything is built. Fold in Cofounder's landscape scan under "who else is here."
- Pass: Oscar can say the first customer and the first task in one sentence, the repair log has ten entries, and the contract question has an answer.
- Cost: 0. Gate: none. Kill: the contract forbids it and cannot be renegotiated; then the first customer changes (small fabricators, a Fab Lab), the ladder stays.

### Stage 1: One bench, three capabilities, two arm classes (2026-11-17 to 2027-02-28; hard target 2027-01-31)
Revised 11:10. Stage 1 is split so that the cheap arm proves what a cheap arm can prove, and the stiff arm is bought on evidence, not on faith.

**Stage 1 software, before any hardware arrives (from 2026-11-17, cost 0).** Install LeRobot on the Mac or the box and run the simulated examples. Build the bare interface: cameras, module commands and an arm API, the AA-Robotics shape, so any model can drive the bench and every run is logged. Build the CAD-from-photo pipeline and test it on the Stage 0 repair log without a printer: ten parts, agent writes the CAD, Oscar checks dimensions against the drawing. Design the box frame, the camera mounts, the tool dock and the first fixtures as print files. This is the half that frontier models speed up, and it is finished before the first parcel.

**Stage 1a, hobby arm (hardware arrival to about six weeks after).**
- Do: build one SO-101 leader and follower pair (official files and bill of materials: https://github.com/TheRobotStudio/SO-ARM100; D-012). Add the first fabrication module, a desktop 3D printer (Oscar's own if he has access, else D-013), a soldering station, two cameras and a light panel in a fixed frame, MicroFactory's own lesson. Week one, before any task: measure the follower's repeatability and settling time at three points of the workspace with a dial indicator and the cameras, twenty repeats each, and write the numbers at the top of the protocol. Then three tests, same arm, same agent:
  - (a) Repair part: photo plus dimensions of a broken bracket, knob or cover from the Stage 0 repair log, agent writes the CAD, printer makes it, arm unloads, deburrs and fits it to the test fixture. Twenty unseen parts, fixtures designed to the measured repeatability (clearance fits, chamfers, jigs), Kulakov's rule: build the jig for the awkward step.
  - (b) Soldering: a through-hole practice kit, attempted and scored per joint by someone other than Oscar. No pass line is claimed on this arm; the number is reported as what a hobby arm does.
  - (c) Yard bench task from the Stage 0 list (my pick: cut a cable to a marked length and strip both ends), run two ways on the same fifty unseen trials: a SmolVLA fine-tune from `lerobot/smolvla_base` on fifty demonstrations (https://huggingface.co/blog/smolvla), and frontier-model-written code with a small aligned dataset (Kulakov's System 2 plus System 1 method). Both numbers published.
  Learning programme alongside, Fionn's point: LeRobot docs, the π0.5 paper (https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16054), the AMP1 paper, Gershenfeld's fab lab material, and the @ihorbeaver production threads in the sweep.
- Pass (hard targets, treated as reasonable): (a) 15 of 20 parts accepted against the drawing by a fitter; (c) 80 percent over fifty unseen trials on at least one of the two methods; (b) reported, not passed. The first filmed end-to-end run (photo in, fitted part out) within fourteen days of the hardware arriving. All filmed, scored by someone other than Oscar. Calibration: SmolVLA's authors report 78.3 percent on real SO-100 tasks; frontier-model harnesses take about 30 minutes per task against 20 seconds for a human (https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/aa-robotics), so time per part is logged and reported, not hidden.
- Cost: arm pair (D-012, ceiling 400 EUR) plus printer (D-013) plus a practice kit, a dial indicator and filament. Gate 2 for each purchase. Kill, fast version: if the week-one repeatability number is worse than the fit tolerance of every part on the repair log, stop 1a's test (a), keep (c) and the learning, and raise D-014 at once. Kill, slow version: after six weeks no test is above half its pass line and the logs do not say whether the arm or the agent is the cause; then the next step is a technical cofounder, not more hardware.

**Stage 1b, stiff arm (on D-014, after 1a's numbers).**
- Do: one 6-axis arm with ±0.1 mm repeatability or better on the same bench and the same interface. Quoted candidates and prices in section 2; my pick is the AgileX PiPER at 2,400 EUR incl. VAT. Tool-fixed grippers swapped whole (gripper, soldering iron, driver, deburring tool), a 300 USD class screw feeder rather than a clever grasp. Same three tests, same protocol, the SO-101 numbers kept as the baseline.
- Pass: (a) 18 of 20 parts; (b) 90 percent of joints; (c) 90 percent over fifty unseen trials; a press fit done. ## 6. Risks, stated plainly

- **Capital, and the scale trap.** Each rung costs roughly an order of magnitude more hardware than the last. The ladder is built so each rung is paid by the one before it: Stage 1 by Oscar, Stage 3 by a small raise or the ESA BIC, Stage 5 by the pre-seed and a pilot customer. A rung started before the previous one has paid is the way this dies.
- **Feedstock.** A factory in a village with no stock is a decoration. The container's feedstock plan comes from the demand log, and Stage 6 is where local material enters, through partners. On Mars, feedstock is the whole problem; the gypsum-strength bricks above show how early that field is.
- **Field reliability.** Lab rates do not survive dust, heat and an untrained operator. Every stage runs unattended tests and reports time per part with the pass rate. Stage 5's pass line includes a self-repair.
- **Day-job conflict.** A bench automation venture next to his employer's trade, with the employer's customers as a later market, needs the contract read now and a written agreement before any pilot or approach. Stage 0, not negotiable.
- **Hype.** Published rates are on tasks the authors chose, and frontier-model control is slow. Stage 1 replaces belief with three measured rates and the time per part.
- **The cheap-hardware trap (added 11:10).** The founder closest to us says the model needs arms that do not wobble. Twelve weeks spent teaching a hobby arm a task it is mechanically unable to do would be the cheapest way to lose a quarter. Stage 1a measures the arm before it trusts it, claims no soldering pass line on it, and has a written trigger for the stiff arm.
- **A competitor with a head start (added 11:10).** MicroFactory is fifteen months ahead on the arm cell, has paying deployments, a trained model, and an AI integrator, and states the same endgame. Competing on the bench is lost before it starts. The plan competes on what he does not do (raw stock in, no-resupply sites, Europe and naval access, the demand log) and keeps buying his cell as an option. If he ships a fabrication module and a container before Stage 3, the kill question is asked then, with his prices next to ours.
- **Open-world control (added 11:10).** He also says direct LLM control is slow and unreliable outside a constrained, repetitive cell. The plan agrees and never depends on the opposite: every rung is a boxed cell with fixtures, and the container is a row of them. Any stage that drifts toward "the robot figures it out on the floor" is off the ladder.
- **Safety and certification.** A cell on a floor is a machine under EU Regulation 2023/1230, applying from 20 January 2027 (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1230/oj); whoever substantially modifies a machine counts as its manufacturer under Article 18. A container shipped abroad adds the destination's rules. A defence customer adds export control. The regulation-procurement role maps each before the stage that needs it.
- **Focus.** Three ideas in three days. D-011 makes this the sole focus; Digest and crowd control are parked with revisit dates in Cofounder's files.
- **Team.** Fionn's advice stands: wait on a startup with Paul and Mathieu until team fit is clear. Stage 1 answers whether Oscar is the builder; Stage 3 needs a second pair of hands either way.
